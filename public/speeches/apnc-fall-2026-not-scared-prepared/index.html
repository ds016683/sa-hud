Where we are

Artificial intelligence has arrived in behavioral health.

The conversation about it swings between hype and fear. Neither serves the field. The question in this room is not whether AI arrives. It is who sets the terms when it does.

Fall Conference 2026
A clear-eyed look

What AI is, what it is not, and what it cannot do without you.

What it is

Pattern recognition at scale over data someone chose to collect. Fast, tireless, and confident whether it is right or wrong.

What it is not

Judgment. A license. A relationship with a client. A substitute for the person in the room.

What it cannot do without the expertise of addiction professionals

Know what the data means in this field, in this county, this year. That knowledge is yours.

Fall Conference 2026
On the sidelines

The preconceptions that keep the field on the sidelines.

It will replace us

It cannot. It can replace the parts of the day that were never the work.

It is not for a field like ours

A field built on relationships has the most to gain from knowing its own ground.

It is too risky to touch

The risk is real. Standing aside does not make it smaller, it makes it someone else's call.

Someone else will sort it out

They will. Vendors, payers and policymakers, without the people who know the work.

The cost

What gets missed when fear drives the conversation.

The decisions get made anyway. The tools get bought anyway. What gets left out is the field's own knowledge: what the numbers mean, what matters, what would actually help on a Tuesday afternoon.

Two different things

Single-tool AI versus integrated data platforms.

Single-tool AI, such as chatbots

  • One question, one answer, no ground truth underneath it
  • Knows the internet. Does not know North Carolina
  • Confident either way
  • Useful for drafting. Not for deciding

Integrated data platforms

  • The field's own data, connected and refreshed
  • Questions answered against ground truth
  • Insight you can act on and defend
  • Built with the workforce, for the workforce

What you leave with

Three things you can do on Monday.

  1. Describe how connected, real-time data on the workforce, funding, policy, and community conditions can inform daily practice, program decisions, and advocacy.
  2. Identify common misconceptions about artificial intelligence in behavioral health and distinguish what AI tools can and cannot do without professional expertise.
  3. Distinguish single-tool AI solutions, such as chatbots, from integrated data platforms, and describe what platform-based approaches mean for North Carolina's addiction workforce.
